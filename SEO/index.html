Section 01

What is a Search Engine?

A search engine is a program that searches for and identifies items in a database that correspond to keywords or characters specified by the user — used especially for finding particular sites on the World Wide Web.

Search Engine Optimization (SEO) is the practice of making your website as easy to understand, parse, and interpret as possible for these programs — so that your pages appear higher in search results without paying for ads.

💡

Google's rule for SEO: "Build your pages to be great for humans, and the robots will love them too."

Examples of Search Engines

🌐

Google

General web search — the most used search engine globally with over 90% market share.

🍳

Allrecipes.com

A domain-specific search engine for recipes — shows that any well-indexed database is a search engine.

🔢

Wolfram Alpha

A computational search engine for math and science — returns calculated answers, not links.

🎞️

Giphy

A visual search engine for animated GIFs — searches by keyword and returns media.

🗃️

Archive.org

The Wayback Machine — search engine for historical snapshots of websites.

🖼️

TinEye

A reverse image search engine — find where an image appears on the web.

Google's Many Search Engines

Google itself is not just one search engine — it has many specialised indexes:

Web News Images Videos Books Scholar Flights Recipes Shopping Maps / Local

Each of these indexes different types of content. When you optimize for SEO, you're not just targeting one — good SEO can help your content appear across multiple Google surfaces.

Section 02

How SEO Works

When you publish a webpage, Google sends out automated programs called crawlers (also called spiders or bots) to discover and read your page. The crawler follows links from page to page, reading the HTML content — not the visual design.

Once crawled, Google indexes the page — storing it in a massive database. When someone searches, Google's algorithm scores all indexed pages against the search query and returns the most relevant results.

1

Crawl

Google's bots discover your page by following links across the web.

→
2

Index

The page is read, understood, and stored in Google's database.

→
3

Rank

When someone searches, Google scores your page against hundreds of factors and decides where it appears.

⚠️

Important: Search engines do not see CSS. They read your HTML. A beautifully designed page with poor HTML structure will rank worse than a plain page with excellent semantic HTML.

Why Does This Matter to You?

As a web developer or designer

You want your company, product, service or idea to appear to as many potential customers as possible — and to pay the least amount possible to do so. Paid ads stop working the moment you stop paying. Good SEO keeps working for years.

For your personal portfolio

After graduation you will be selling yourself as a professional. You want employers and clients searching for web design talent in Calgary to be able to find you — without you spending money on ads. SEO is how you make that happen.

Section 03

On-Page SEO

On-page SEO covers everything directly within your control — how you write your HTML, what content you publish, and how your page is structured. These are the factors a web developer has the most influence over.

HTML Structure

Google reads your HTML to understand what a page is about. Using semantic, well-structured HTML makes this easier.

❌ Bad — not semantic
<div class="big-text">
    Calgary Web Developer
</div>
<div class="smaller">
    I build websites
</div>
<div>
    <img src="me.jpg">
</div>

Google has no idea what "big-text" or "smaller" means. The image has no description.

✅ Good — semantic HTML
<h1>Calgary Web Developer</h1>
<p>I build responsive websites
using HTML, CSS and JavaScript.
</p>
<img src="me.jpg"
     alt="Prashant Sharma,
     web developer in Calgary">

Google understands the heading hierarchy, the body text, and what the image shows.

Key On-Page Factors

📝

Page Title — <title>

The most important on-page SEO element. Appears in the browser tab and as the blue link in Google results. Should include your primary keyword and be under 60 characters.

<title>Calgary Web Developer | Prashant Sharma</title>
📋

Meta Description — <meta name="description">

The short summary shown under the page title in Google results. Doesn't directly affect ranking but affects click-through rate. Keep under 160 characters.

<meta name="description" content="Calgary-based web developer specializing in responsive HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Available for freelance projects.">
🔤

Heading Hierarchy — <h1> to <h6>

Use one <h1> per page — it tells Google the main topic. Subheadings (<h2>, <h3>) structure the content like chapters in a book.

<h1>Web Development Portfolio</h1> <h2>Projects</h2> <h3>Dexter's Laboratory Fan Page</h3>
🖼️

Image Alt Text — alt attribute

Google cannot see images — it reads the alt attribute to understand what they show. Every image should have a descriptive alt text. This also makes your page accessible to screen readers.

<!-- Bad --> <img src="project.jpg" alt="image"> <!-- Good --> <img src="project.jpg" alt="Screenshot of responsive restaurant website on mobile and desktop">
🔗

Meaningful Link Text

The text inside a link tells Google what the linked page is about. Avoid "click here" — use descriptive text that makes sense out of context.

<!-- Bad --> <a href="/projects">Click here</a> <!-- Good --> <a href="/projects">View my web development projects</a>
📱

Mobile Optimization

Google uses mobile-first indexing — it crawls and ranks the mobile version of your page, not the desktop version. If your site isn't responsive, it will rank lower. This is why responsive design is an SEO requirement, not just a nice-to-have.

<!-- Required in every HTML file --> <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1.0">
⚡

Page Load Speed

Google favours fast-loading pages. Large uncompressed images are the most common culprit. Use modern formats like WebP, compress images before uploading, and avoid unnecessary JavaScript.

🔒

URL Structure

URLs should be short, readable, and include keywords where possible. Avoid random numbers or query strings in URLs.

❌ yoursite.com/page?id=4821 ✅ yoursite.com/projects/restaurant-website
Section 04

Off-Page SEO

Off-page SEO refers to factors outside your direct control — how the rest of the web references and links to your site. These are sometimes called organic SEO factors.

The most important off-page factor is backlinks — other websites linking to yours. Google treats a link from another site as a "vote of confidence." A link from a high-authority site (like a university, a major news outlet, or a well-known industry blog) carries much more weight than a link from an unknown site.

🔗

Backlinks — Quality over Quantity

One link from a reputable source like a government site or university is worth more than 100 links from low-quality directories. Focus on creating content worth linking to, and building genuine relationships with other professionals in your field.

📱

Social Media Presence

Posting on LinkedIn, GitHub, or other platforms and linking back to your portfolio builds signals that your site is active and relevant. Social shares don't directly affect ranking, but they drive traffic — which does.

🕰️

Domain Age and History

Older domains with consistent history tend to rank better. Once you pick a domain name for your portfolio, don't change it — changing your domain resets your accumulated authority.

🌍

Domain Country Code

A .ca domain will rank higher in Canadian search results. A .co.uk domain ranks better in the UK. For a Canadian employer looking for a Calgary developer, a .ca domain signals local relevance.

Section 06

5 Biggest SEO Mistakes

1

Focusing on SEO before defining your value proposition

If you don't know what your site is for or who it's for, no amount of keyword optimisation will help. Know your purpose first — then optimise for it.

2

Focusing on SEO before focusing on users

Google's algorithm is designed to reward pages that genuinely help users. A page optimised for humans will naturally score well with robots. Optimising for robots first usually produces pages that feel hollow and rank poorly anyway.

3

Spending time on hacks and workarounds

Keyword stuffing, buying links, and other shortcuts may produce a short-term boost but Google detects and penalises these patterns. A penalty can push a site off the first 10 pages of results — sometimes permanently.

4

Creating hidden content to trick crawlers

Hiding text using display: none, white text on white backgrounds, or showing different content to bots than to users is called cloaking. Google considers this a violation of its guidelines and will de-index the site.

5

Not keeping up with Google's algorithm changes

Google updates its algorithm thousands of times per year. Following Google Search Central keeps you informed of what is being rewarded and penalised — ignoring it means strategies that worked last year may actively hurt you today.

Section 09

Web Analytics

Analytics is the discovery, interpretation, and communication of meaningful patterns in data. Web analytics specifically tracks how users find and interact with your website — giving you the data to make informed decisions about design, content, and strategy.

📊

Measure

Collect data on how users interact with your site — page views, time on page, clicks, form submissions.

📋

Report

Organise the data into meaningful summaries — weekly traffic reports, conversion rate trends, top-performing pages.

🔍

Analyze

Ask why — why did traffic drop? Why do users leave the contact page? Use data to answer these questions and make changes.

What Can You Learn From Analytics?

👥

How many people visit my website — and how many are new vs returning?

🌍

Where do my visitors live — country, city, language?

🔗

Which websites send traffic to my site?

📱

How many users are on mobile vs desktop?

📄

Which pages are the most popular?

🛒

How many visitors converted into customers or enquiries?

🗺️

Where do visitors go after landing on the homepage?

❤️

What content do users engage with the most?

Data-Driven Design

Analytics data directly informs design and content decisions. Instead of guessing what users want, you can look at real behaviour and redesign accordingly.

  • If 80% of your users are on mobile but your site is desktop-first — prioritise mobile in your next redesign.
  • If users consistently leave your contact page without submitting — maybe the form is too long or the CTA is unclear.
  • If a blog post drives 60% of your traffic — write more content like it.
  • If you're spending hours making Facebook graphics but they drive zero traffic — redirect that time elsewhere.
  • If users are arriving on your site from a specific Google search — make sure that page answers that query thoroughly.
